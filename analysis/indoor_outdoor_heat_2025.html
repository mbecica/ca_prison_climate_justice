Indoor vs. Outdoor Heat in California Prisons, 2025

CDCR State Prisons · 2025 · California Prison Climate Justice, UC Berkeley DCRP

AB 2499 requires CDCR to install continuous indoor temperature monitors at three prisons by July 2027 and post data publicly each week. The bill sets 85°F as the threshold above which days and hours must be identified and reported. This analysis uses the 2025 indoor temperature data reported by CDCR at 78°F, alongside daily outdoor gridMET data, to address two questions relevant to that pilot: do building characteristics help suggest which three facilities should be monitored for the pilot, and what does comparing indoor to outdoor temperature data tell us about heat exposure at each type of building? Pilot location or intervention suggestions require a separate analysis that incorporates vulnerability data about the people inside each facility; this report focuses on the built environment as one step in estimating total heat risk to inform costs. Technical findings about the cluster analysis and variable relationships are at the end of this document.

  • The eight facilities with the oldest buildings and little mechanical cooling (Cluster A) are the only group where outdoor temperatures significantly predict indoor heat — but CDCR's 90°F operational threshold fires near-zero times per year at these facilities, missing the relationship entirely.
  • The five facilities with full mechanical AC (Cluster B) have largely decoupled indoor from outdoor conditions, but individual cases like CAL — 137 indoor days above 78°F despite 100% AC coverage — suggest equipment performance during extreme events is the key unknown.
  • The 18 facilities with predominantly evaporative cooling (Cluster C) show a fourteen-fold range in indoor outcomes across similar outdoor climates, with no detectable outdoor-indoor relationship. Equipment performance variation and urban heat retention at high-UHI facilities are two distinct patterns that outdoor data cannot distinguish.

Because cooling infrastructure varies widely across the 31 facilities, a single system-wide correlation between indoor and outdoor temperatures is not meaningful. Facilities were grouped into three clusters using k-means on nine exposure variables. Each cluster has a distinct relationship between outdoor conditions and indoor heat.

What outdoor temperature data tells us — and misses — at each facility type

Cluster A, the Thermal Trap, is the only cluster where outdoor temperature significantly predicts indoor heat. The correlation between outdoor and indoor days above 78°F is r = +0.806 (p = 0.016) across these eight facilities. That relationship is detectable at 78°F, but CDCR's Stage I threshold triggers at 90°F outdoor — a temperature these facilities averaged only 11 days per year in 2025. PBSP reported 63 indoor days above 78°F despite outdoor temperatures rarely exceeding 65°F. CTF in Salinas and CMC in San Luis Obispo show the same pattern. These are the oldest buildings in the system (avg built 1946), and low UHI scores (avg 0.05) rule out outdoor overnight warming as the mechanism. Building thermal mass likely absorbs solar radiation and releases it slowly, generating indoor heat independently of outdoor peak temperature.

Cluster B, Full Mechanical AC, has refrigerant cooling that keeps indoor temperatures largely independent of outdoor peaks. Five desert-climate facilities averaged 174 outdoor 78°F days and 143 outdoor 90°F days in 2025, but only 68 indoor days above 78°F. The correlation between outdoor and indoor days is r = −0.173 (p = 0.78). What outdoor data cannot tell you is whether that independence holds during extreme heat events or whether equipment runs near its limits. CAL in Calipatria reported 137 indoor days despite 100% AC coverage — a gap the cluster average obscures.

Cluster C, Predominantly Evaporative Cooling, has no detectable outdoor-indoor relationship (r = +0.104, p = 0.68) across 18 facilities. There are two patterns in the facilities in this cluster: variation in evaporative cooling equipment performance, and urban heat retention at facilities with high urban heat island intensity. Outdoor data cannot distinguish between them.

  • Wide variation between similar facilities: facilities with similar outdoor climates and the same cooling type show a fourteen-fold range in indoor days above 78°F. MCSP logged 11 indoor days against 138 outdoor 78°F days in 2025, while ASP logged 132 indoor days against 162 outdoor. That variation is not visible in outdoor temperature records.
  • Potential heat retention at high-UHI facilities: SOL, COR, NKSP, SATF, CIW, and others within the cluster are in areas with high urban heat island effects. Urban surfaces likely retain heat overnight, raising the indoor baseline the next day regardless of outdoor peaks. Outdoor overnight temperature minimums carry no explanatory power within the high-UHI subset (see methodology), pointing to structural heat retention that outdoor data cannot measure.

What indoor monitoring would add

For Cluster A, the Thermal Trap, outdoor 78°F days significantly predict indoor exposure (r = +0.806, p = 0.016), so continuous monitoring at the 85°F indoor trigger would better identify at-risk days than CDCR's 90°F outdoor operational threshold — which triggers near-zero times per year at PBSP, CTF, CMC, and SQ.

For Cluster B, Full Mechanical AC, monitoring would quantify what AC protection actually delivers day-to-day. ISP had 1 reported indoor day above 78°F against 192 outdoor 90°F days, but continuous data would show near-misses, fluctuations during peak events, and how close equipment runs to its limits. CAL reported 137 indoor days despite 100% AC coverage; continuous data would show whether that reflects equipment failures, specific buildings within the facility, or sustained overload during extreme heat.

For Cluster C, Predominantly Evaporative Cooling, monitoring would distinguish between the two mechanisms that outdoor data cannot separate. At facilities where the primary driver is cooling equipment performance, indoor sensors would show whether evaporative systems are working and whether they degrade during heat events — information the fourteen-fold variation in indoor outcomes across similar outdoor climates makes clear is unavailable. At facilities where urban heat retention is the likely mechanism, continuous data would show whether indoor temperatures recover overnight or stay elevated, capturing the cumulative heat load that daily threshold counts miss. Both questions require the same instrument: sensors inside the building.

Methodology

Indoor temperature data comes from the CDCR Air Cooling Pilot Program Supplemental Report (January 2026, Table 1), which reports the number of days each housing unit type exceeded 78°F between May and October 2025. Outdoor temperature data comes from gridMET daily maximum temperature (tmmx), extracted at each facility's coordinates for the same window. Outdoor days above 78°F and above 90°F were computed from daily facility-level records. Facility attributes — elevation, normalized UHI intensity, census tract hot-nights percentage, year opened, and mechanical and evaporative cooling shares — come from CDCR facility records, EPA EnviroAtlas / NLCD 2019, CalEnviroScreen 4.0, USGS National Elevation Dataset, and the CDCR Air Cooling Supplemental Report Table 2.

Facilities were grouped using k-means (k=3) applied to nine standardized (z-score) features: year opened, percent mechanical AC, percent evaporative cooling, outdoor days above 78°F, indoor days above 78°F, normalized UHI, elevation (m), latitude, and census tract hot-nights percentage. Two facilities (CCI, PVSP) had missing UHI values and were assigned 0.0 before standardization. The random seed was set to 0. k=3 was selected based on the elbow plot (see analysis/elbow_kmeans.png); the largest percentage drop in within-cluster sum of squares occurs at k=3→4 (24.3%), but k=3 produces more interpretable groupings and avoids splitting the evaporative cooling and urban heat retention facilities into separate clusters when their monitoring implications can be addressed together. PCA was applied to the same standardized feature matrix for visualization; PC1 and PC2 together explain 47.5% of variance.

Pearson r between indoor 78°F days and outdoor temperature variables was computed within each cluster and system-wide. Cluster A (Thermal Trap, n=8) is the only cluster with a statistically significant correlation (r = +0.806, p = 0.016, two-tailed). All other cluster-level r values are descriptive; sample sizes of 5–18 are too small for reliable inference on the non-significant results. Outdoor overnight minimum temperature (gridMET tmin, May–October 2025) was separately tested as a predictor of indoor days within the high-UHI subset of Cluster C to assess whether overnight outdoor conditions could explain the indoor-outdoor divergence observed there. Average overnight tmin correlates with indoor days at r = +0.001 and nights above 65°F at r = −0.284; neither is statistically significant.

Limitations

Indoor temperature data. CDCR's indoor temperature figures come from the January 2026 Air Cooling Pilot Program Supplemental Report, which records the number of days each housing unit type exceeded 78°F between May and October 2025. This is a single-threshold annual count derived from facility self-report, not a continuous sensor record. It cannot distinguish between a facility that briefly exceeded 78°F on many days and one that sustained high temperatures for hours at a time, and it is subject to the reporting inconsistencies that motivate AB 2499 in the first place.

Single year of data. All temperature variables — indoor days, outdoor days, and tmin — are from 2025 only. Cluster assignments and correlations reflect one summer's conditions. Unusually hot or mild conditions in 2025 at specific facilities could shift cluster membership; a multi-year average would produce more stable groupings.

Threshold mismatch. CDCR reports indoor data at 78°F, CDCR's Stage I operational threshold is 90°F outdoor, and AB 2499's proposed reporting trigger is 85°F indoor. None of these thresholds are directly comparable, and the analysis uses 78°F throughout because it is the only threshold for which indoor data exists.

UHI and hotnights are tract-level proxies. Urban heat island intensity and census tract hot-nights percentage are assigned at the census tract level, not the facility parcel. Facilities in large or heterogeneous tracts may have UHI or hot-nights values that poorly represent conditions at their specific location. UHI values are missing for CCI and PVSP and were set to 0.0 before standardization.

Cooling infrastructure shares are building counts, not capacity. Mechanical AC, evaporative, and ventilation-only percentages reflect the share of housing buildings with each system type, not installed capacity, equipment age, or condition. A facility coded as 100% evaporative may have poorly maintained or undersized equipment; the data cannot distinguish this from a well-functioning system.

Clustering is unsupervised and k=3 is a choice. The k=3 grouping was selected based on the elbow plot and interpretability of the monitoring narrative. Several facilities — particularly CCI, FOL, and CIM — sit near cluster boundaries and could reasonably be assigned differently. The PCA biplot explains 47.5% of variance on two components; the remaining 52.5% is not visible in that visualization.

Vulnerability data excluded. This report addresses built environment and temperature variables only. Pilot site selection under AB 2499 should also incorporate facility-level vulnerability data: population health status, age distribution, time in cell, and access to cooling respite. A facility in Cluster A with high thermal mass risk may or may not be the highest-priority pilot site once population vulnerability is factored in.
