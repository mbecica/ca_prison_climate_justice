Indoor vs. Outdoor Heat: A Facility-Level Discrepancy Analysis

CDCR State Prisons · 2025 · California Prison Climate Justice, UC Berkeley DCRP

Outdoor temperature thresholds — the standard basis for CDCR heat emergency plans — are a poor predictor of indoor heat conditions inside California state prisons. Across 31 facilities in 2025, the correlation between days with outdoor tmax ≥ 90°F and days with indoor housing unit temperature exceeding 78°F was r = 0.076, effectively zero. A cluster-based analysis reveals a more nuanced picture. Cluster B (old buildings in mild coastal and inland valley climates, n=8) shows the strongest link at r = 0.809 — even modest outdoor heat is amplified by aging thermal mass. Cluster A (predominantly evaporative cooling, moderate outdoor heat, n=10) shows a partial link at r = 0.458 — evaporative systems attenuate but do not eliminate the outdoor signal. Cluster C (100% mechanical AC, n=4) shows r = 0.369, a weak positive relationship driven largely by Calipatria, which faces extreme outdoor heat even with full AC. Most strikingly, Cluster D (high UHI, mixed or insufficient cooling, n=9) shows r = −0.403 — a negative correlation: these facilities face chronic indoor overheating that no longer tracks outdoor peak temperatures at all, driven instead by persistent urban heat island conditions.

This report compares indoor 78°F days (CDCR Air Cooling Pilot Program Supplemental Report, January 2026, Table 1) against outdoor 90°F days (gridMET tmmx, 2025) and mechanical cooling share (CDCR January 2026, Table 2) for all 31 adult institutions included in both datasets.

Key Findings

1. Outdoor thresholds miss the majority of indoor overheating

The near-zero correlation (r = 0.076) means that using outdoor 90°F days as a proxy for indoor heat risk is unreliable across the CDCR system. Of the 2,921 total indoor facility-days above 78°F recorded system-wide in 2025, the majority occurred at facilities that experienced fewer than 100 outdoor days above 90°F — facilities that would not be flagged as high-risk by exterior temperature monitoring alone.

2. Coastal and inland valley facilities show the most severe indoor-to-outdoor ratios

Five facilities — all located in coastal or mild-inland climates — recorded indoor overheating at 20× or more the rate implied by outdoor temperatures:

These facilities would not trigger any outdoor-threshold-based heat emergency plan, yet incarcerated people inside experienced heat above 78°F for 33–115 days in 2025. Solar gain, thermal mass, and poor ventilation in aging building stock appear to drive indoor overheating largely independently of outdoor peak temperatures.

3. Mechanical AC is the only reliable mitigation

Fully air-conditioned facilities show a clear protective effect. ISP (192 outdoor days ≥ 90°F, 100% AC) recorded just 1 indoor day above 78°F. CHCF (75 outdoor days, 100% AC) recorded 0. CEN (194 outdoor days, 100% AC) recorded 42 — high in absolute terms, but a ratio of just 0.22 compared to its extreme outdoor heat burden.

Evaporative cooling does not provide equivalent protection. COR (0% mechanical, 100% evaporative) recorded 159 indoor days — a ratio of 1.43 relative to its 111 outdoor days. ASP, SATF, WSP, and NKSP, all predominantly evaporative, recorded indoor days at or above their outdoor counts.

4. CMF is a notable anomaly

California Medical Facility (CMF) has 53% mechanical cooling yet recorded 135 indoor days above 78°F against only 54 outdoor days (ratio 2.50). This is the highest ratio among facilities with majority mechanical cooling and warrants further investigation — possible explanations include uneven distribution of AC units across housing types, measurement methodology differences in the CDCR report, or a mismatch between the housing units counted as "mechanical" and the units where temperature was recorded.
